Introduction to the IPA

Before diving into individual sounds, learn what the IPA is, how to read it, and why it turns French pronunciation from guesswork into something precise.

01 · Learn

French spelling lies to you — the IPA doesn’t.

French has roughly 36 distinct sounds, but only 26 letters to write them with — and those letters get combined, dropped, and recycled in ways that rarely match how a word actually sounds. eau, au, and o can all represent the exact same vowel. The letter s can be silent, voiced like a z, or voiceless like an s, depending entirely on what’s around it.

The International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA) fixes this by giving every distinct sound in human language its own dedicated symbol. One symbol, one sound, always — no exceptions, no silent letters, no guessing. Once you can read IPA, you can look up any French word and know exactly how it’s pronounced, with zero ambiguity.

02 · Learn

Reading the notation.

IPA transcriptions are written between slashes or square brackets, like /ʃa/ for the French word chat (cat). Each symbol between the slashes corresponds to exactly one sound — here, ʃ (the “sh” sound) followed by a. Two sounds, two symbols, nothing silent.

/ʃa/

chat — cat

Written with 4 letters (c-h-a-t), but only 2 sounds: ʃ and a. The final t is silent — and the IPA simply doesn’t write it, because there’s no sound there to represent.

You’ll sometimes see square brackets [ ] instead of slashes / / in more technical texts — brackets mark the precise, physical sound a speaker actually made, while slashes mark the sound’s abstract role in the language. For learning pronunciation, you can treat them the same way.

03 · Learn

The French sound system, at a glance.

French sounds fall into four families, and the rest of this track covers each one in turn:

12

Oral vowels

i, e, ɛ, a, ɑ, ɔ, o, u, y, ø, œ, ə — more than twice as many as English, including several with no real English equivalent.

4

Nasal vowels

ɑ̃, ɛ̃, ɔ̃, œ̃ — vowels pronounced with air flowing through the nose. English doesn’t have these at all as distinct sounds.

3

Semi-vowels

j, ɥ, w — quick glides between a vowel and a consonant, as in yeux, huit, and oui.

17

Consonants

Mostly familiar, but a handful — especially ʁ, the French R — behave nothing like their English namesakes.
