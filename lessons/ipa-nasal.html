Nasal Vowels

French uses four nasal vowels that don’t exist in English as distinct sounds. Learn to hear and spot them — they change meaning, not just accent.

Lesson Progress 0%

01 · Learn

What makes a vowel nasal.

For an oral vowel, air flows out through your mouth only. For a nasal vowel, the soft palate drops and lets air flow through your nose at the same time — the vowel keeps its basic tongue position but takes on a distinct, resonant quality. English doesn’t use this contrastively at all (English vowels before n or m can sound slightly nasal, but swapping that nasality never changes the word’s meaning the way it does in French).

French has four nasal vowels, and getting them right matters: vin (wine) and vingt (twenty) are distinguished almost entirely by vowel quality, and mixing up bon (good) with banc (bench) is a classic learner slip.

02 · Learn

The four nasal vowel symbols.

Each nasal vowel pairs with an oral vowel you already know — same tongue position, air redirected through the nose.

[ɑ̃]

dans — in

Related to oral [ɑ]. The most open of the four nasal vowels.

[ɛ̃]

vin — wine

Related to oral [ɛ]. Spelled many ways — in, im, ain, ein, yn, ym all land here.

[ɔ̃]

bon — good

Related to oral [ɔ]. Rounded lips, air through the nose.

[œ̃]

un — a, one

Related to oral [œ]. The rarest of the four — many French speakers today merge it into [ɛ̃], so hearing un and in sound identical is common and not a mistake on their part.

03 · Learn

Spelling clues: how to spot a nasal vowel in print.

You can usually predict nasalization from spelling: a vowel letter followed by n or m, when that n/m is not followed by another vowel or doubled, signals a nasal vowel — and the n/m itself is not pronounced as a separate consonant sound.

ɑ̃

an / en / am / em

dans, enfant, chambre, temps

ɛ̃

in / im / ain / ein / yn / ym

vin, important, pain, plein, syndicat

ɔ̃

on / om

bon, nombre

œ̃

un / um

un, parfum

The key exception: when n/m is doubled, or followed by a vowel, nasalization usually disappears and it’s pronounced as a normal consonant instead — compare the nasal vowel in bon to the fully oral, non-nasal vowel in bonne (the final e after the doubled nn breaks the nasalization).

04 · Learn

Classic contrasts to listen for.

A handful of word pairs differ by nasal vowel alone — a great way to train your ear.

ɔ̃ / a

bon vs. banc

good vs. bench — [bɔ̃] vs. [bɑ̃]

ɛ̃ / ɑ̃

vin vs. vent

wine vs. wind — [vɛ̃] vs. [vɑ̃]

A well-known mnemonic phrase packs all four nasal vowels into one sentence: “un bon vin blanc” (a good white wine) runs through [œ̃], [ɔ̃], [ɛ̃], and [ɑ̃] in order — worth saying slowly a few times.
