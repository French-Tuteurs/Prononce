Oral Vowels

French has twelve distinct oral vowels — more than twice as many as English. Learn each symbol, grouped by family, with a real word to anchor it to.

Lesson Progress 0%

01 · Learn

Three things change between vowels.

Every oral vowel in French comes down to three things: how high the tongue sits, how far forward or back it is, and whether the lips are rounded. This lesson groups the twelve vowels by family so the pattern is easier to hear, starting with the ones closest to English.

02 · Learn

Front unrounded vowels.

These four sit at the front of the mouth with the lips spread, not rounded — the closest family to English vowel sounds.

[i]

si — if

High, front, spread lips — tense and bright, like the ee in “see” but shorter and crisper.

[e]

été — summer

High-mid, front, spread lips. No real English equivalent — closer to the vowel in “say” but without the glide at the end.

[ɛ]

fête — party

Lower-mid, front, spread lips. Close to the e in “bed,” a bit more open than [e].

[a]

chat — cat

Low, front, spread lips — the most open vowel in this group, close to the a in “cat.”

03 · Learn

Front rounded vowels.

These three use the exact same tongue positions as [i], [e], and [ɛ] above — the only difference is rounded lips. None of them exist in English, which is exactly why they trip learners up: your tongue already knows what to do, your lips just have to catch up.

[y]

tu — you

Same tongue position as [i] — high and forward — but with lips tightly rounded, as if starting to whistle.

[ø]

peu — a little

Same tongue position as [e], lips rounded. Say [e], then round your lips without moving your tongue.

[œ]

sœur — sister

Same tongue position as [ɛ], lips rounded — a touch more open than [ø].

04 · Learn

Back vowels and the schwa.

The back vowels are produced with the tongue pulled toward the back of the mouth and the lips rounded. The schwa [ə] is the odd one out: short, central, and often dropped entirely in casual speech.

[ɑ]

pâte — dough/pasta

Low and further back than [a], lips relaxed rather than rounded. In most of France today this has largely merged with [a] — worth knowing it exists, but don’t worry about distinguishing it by ear yet.

[ɔ]

comme — like, as

Low-mid, back, rounded. Close to the o in British “hot.”

[o]

eau — water

High-mid, back, rounded — closer to the vowel in “go,” but held steady instead of gliding into a w sound.

[u]

loup — wolf

High and back, tightly rounded lips — close to “oo” in “food.” Not the same symbol as [y] — [u] is rounded-and-back, [y] is rounded-and-front.

[ə]

le — the

Short, central, relaxed — the “uh” sound. Frequently dropped in fast speech: je ne sais pas is often said more like “j’n’sais pas.”
